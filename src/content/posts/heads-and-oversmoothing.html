What does adding more attention heads actually do in graph neural networks? This post develops an energy-based view of multi-head attention on graphs and uses it to separate two phenomena: oversquashing (long-range information being compressed away) and oversmoothing (node representations becoming too similar). We'll see that head count behaves very differently in these two regimes: in short, more heads do not fix oversquashing under a fixed width budget, but they may help with oversmoothing when the heads diversify in the right way.

The shape of a graph attention block

Let a graph be \(G=(V,E)\) with \(n=|V|\) nodes and no self-loops. At layer \(t\), each node \(v\) holds a feature vector \(h_v^{(t)}\in\mathbb{R}^p\); stacking rows gives \(H^{(t)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}\). A generic neighborhood multi-head attention layer with \(H\) heads computes

$$ g_v^{(t,h)} = \sum_{u\in\mathcal N(v)} \alpha_{vu}^{(t,h)}V^{(t,h)}h_u^{(t)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}, \qquad z_v^{(t)} = [g_v^{(t,1)}|\dots|g_v^{(t,H)}] $$

and then updates

$$ h_v^{(t+1)} = \sigma\Big(W_O^{(t)} z_v^{(t)} + h_v^{(t)}\Big) $$

Here \(V^{(t,h)}\) are the per-head value maps, \(W_O^{(t)}\) mixes the concatenated heads back to width \(p\), \(\alpha^{(t,h)}_{vu}\) are normalized neighbor weights, and \(\sigma\) is a pointwise nonlinearity (e.g., ReLU or leaky-ReLU). 4 4 When differentiating through a layer to bound sensitivity, we "freeze" the \(\alpha^{(t,h)}\) coefficients. This isolates transport through the value maps and residual path without backpropagating through the attention kernel itself. 4 When differentiating through a layer to bound sensitivity, we "freeze" the \(\alpha^{(t,h)}\) coefficients. We will index oversmoothing in the features with the (edge-averaged) Dirichlet energy:

$$ \mathcal{E}\big(H^{(t)}\big) \coloneqq \frac{1}{2|E|}\sum_{(u,v)\in E}\big|h_u^{(t)}-h_v^{(t)}\big|_2^2 $$

Two simple facts about multi-head concatenation will drive our story:

  1. Additivity. If \(Z=[\,Z^{(1)}|\cdots|Z^{(H)}\,]\) concatenates the per-head outputs, then $$\mathcal{E}(Z) \;=\; \sum_{h=1}^H \mathcal{E}\big(Z^{(h)}\big).$$
  2. Operator budgets. Heads contribute to the same effective mixing budget; which norm you use changes how budgets add (linearly in \(L_1\), in root-sum-squares in \(L_2\), etc.). 5 5 For example, if \(A=\operatorname{Concat}(A_1,\dots,A_H)\) then \(\|A\|_2^2\le \sum_h\|A_h\|_2^2\) by Cauchy–Schwarz. Head count doesn't fix oversquashing (under a fixed-width budget)

Oversquashing is the exponential loss of influence that faraway nodes have on a target node. We define the node-to-node influence after $t$ layers as:

$$ J^{(t)}_{v\leftarrow u} \coloneqq \Big\lVert \frac{\partial h_v^{(t)}}{\partial h_u^{(0)}} \Big\rVert_{L_1} $$

We say the model oversquashes if there exist \(C<\infty\) and \(\eta\in(0,1)\) such that for all \(u,v\):

$$ \sup_{t\ge \operatorname{dist}(u,v)} J^{(t)}_{v\leftarrow u} \le C\eta^{\operatorname{dist}(u,v)} $$

We can bounds a layer’s one-step sensitivity by a nonnegative “transport” matrix \(B^{(t)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\):

$$ \Big\lVert \frac{\partial h_v^{(t+1)}}{\partial h_u^{(t)}} \Big\rVert_{L_1} \le c_\sigma\big(\mathbb{I}_{u=v} + B^{(t)}_{vu}\big), \qquad B^{(t)}_{vu}\coloneqq L_O^{(t)} \sum_{h=1}^H \alpha_{vu}^{(t,h)} L_V^{(t,h)} $$

with layerwise Lipschitz surrogates \(L_V^{(t,h)}\) for the value maps, \(L_O^{(t)}\) for the output mixer, and \(c_\sigma\) for the nonlinearity. Summing a row gives

$$ \sum_{u} B^{(t)}_{vu} \le L_O^{(t)}\Lambda^{(t)}, \qquad \Lambda^{(t)}\coloneqq\sum_{h=1}^H L_V^{(t,h)}. $$

Let \(S^{(t)}\coloneqq I+B^{(t)}\) and \(\tau^{(t)}\coloneqq\|S^{(t)}\|_{L_\infty}\le 1+L_O^{(t)}\Lambda^{(t)}\). Chaining across \(m\) layers yields a worst-case bound controlled by the product \(\prod_{t=0}^{m-1}\tau^{(t)}\). Two regimes emerge:

  • Fixed-budget (constant effective width). If the total width \(p=\sum_h d_h\) is fixed and the per-head maps are scaled so that \(\Lambda^{(t)}\) does not grow with \(H\), then \(\tau^{(t)}\) is unchanged. Increasing head count does not improve the worst-case oversquashing rate.
  • Growing-width (adding heads increases \(p\)). If you increase \(H\) by adding dimensions while keeping each \(d_h\) roughly constant, \(L_O^{(t)}\) typically scales with \(p\) 6 6 This is the same conclusion that Di Giovanni et al. reaches in this paper. 6 This is the same conclusion that Di Giovanni et al. reaches in this paper. , pushing \(\tau^{(t)}\) upward; this can worsen the bound. More heads without care can compound mixing rather than alleviate bottlenecks.

I now had a lot of arguments against my hypothesis: oversmoothing would not change with the heads, and I was now aware that previous studies regarding heads on transformers actually showed that you could prune aggressively trained heads and maintain good performances7 7 See Are Sixteen Heads Really Better than One? 7 See Are Sixteen Heads Really Better than One? . However, I still wanted to see if oversmoothing might benefit from an increase in headcount.

Head count can help with oversmoothing

Oversmoothing is the drift toward low-energy (over-smoothed) features as layers accumulate. Here, we'll see how more heads can actually (maybe?) help.

$$ \mathcal{E}(Z) = \sum_{h=1}^H \mathcal{E}\big(g^{(h)}\big) = \underbrace{\sum_{h=1}^H \big(1-2\widehat{\rho}-h\big)S_h}_{\displaystyle \mathsf{Div}\ \text{(diversity index)}} $$

\(\widehat{\rho}_h\) measures how aligned a head is across edges. Anti-alignment (negative \(\widehat{\rho}_h\)) increases energy; strong alignment (positive \(\widehat{\rho}_h\)) decreases it. The block's energy is literally a head-wise sum of "magnitude" \(S_h\) times "anisotropy" \(1-2\widehat{\rho}_h\).

Under a mild "non-positive correlation" assumption: heads that overlap more do not systematically carry larger power, one obtains a simple lower bound

$$ \mathsf{Div}(H) \ge S_{\text{tot}}(H)\big(1-2\bar\rho_H\big) \qquad S_{\text{tot}}(H)=\sum_{h=1}^H S_h,\ \ \bar\rho_H=\tfrac1H\sum_h\widehat\rho_h $$

This also allows us to show that $H \mapsto \mathsf{Div}(H)$ will be increasing with $H$; which was our intuition from the beginning. Hence, adding a head cannot hurt the diversity index,and therefore cannot decrease the block energy, whenever either (a) heads specialize so that the average overlap drops, or (b) the total floor increases (e.g., better utilization of width across heads). Unlike the oversquashing regime (dominated by Lipschitz/budget locality), oversmoothing is ameliorated by head diversification at fixed width. 11 11 Among all pairings of $\{S_h\}$ and $\{\widehat{\rho}_h\}$ with fixed marginals, $\sum_h \widehat{\rho}_h S_h$ is maximized by co-monotone pairing and minimized by anti-monotone pairing (rearrangement inequality). Thus $\mathsf{Div}$ is maximized by pairing large floors with slight overlaps, formalizing the specialization effect. Closing thoughts

Sadly, head count is not a magic bullet for long-range communication on graphs; that's an oversquashing problem largely governed by mixing budgets and graph geometry. But for oversmoothing, head count might be a lever when used to diversify: push heads toward complementary directions (or bands), keep their total power stable, and guard against low-frequency collapse. Can we directly use this to upgrade our usual loss functions? While some papers investigated that a few years back, this has never seen widespread adoption.
